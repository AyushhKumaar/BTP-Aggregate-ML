A B S T R A C T
Construction waste is characterized by heterogeneous type, irregular morphology, and complex 
internal architecture. The primary constituents include recycled mortar (RM), recycled brick 
(RB), and recycled concrete (RC). To systematically investigate the comprehensive influence of 
different particle types and particle sizes on the breakage behavior of construction waste parti­
cles, a series of single-particle compression tests was conducted. The characteristic breakage 
strength and strength variability were quantitatively evaluated using the Weibull distribution 
function. Fractal dimension analysis was performed by comparing gradation curves before and 
after breakage. According to the experiments, the particle size and type have a coupled effect on 
single particle breakage behaviors of construction waste. The Weibull modulus (m) and fractal 
dimensions (D) show that with the increase of particle size, the breakage strength dispersion of 
RM and RB decreases, whereas that of RC increases. Particles with the lower breakage strength 
exhibited a greater propensity for fragmentation, demonstrating pronounced fractal character­
istics consistent with established construction waste fractal models. Based on the force- 
displacement characteristics and fragmentation patterns, three distinct breakage modes were 
identified: intermediate splitting, mixed breakage, and bulk breakage. The proportions of 
different breakage modes varied with particle types and sizes. In general, as particle size in­
creases, the dominant breakage mode of RM and RB transitions from bulk breakage to mixed 
breakage, whereas intermediate splitting mode becomes the predominant breakage mode of RC 
particles. The research results can provide technical insights into the breakage characteristics of 
construction waste materials and offer potential guidance for engineering applications.

particle geometry, and size distribution [14,20], which is generally investigated through single-particle compression test. [26])

evolution. However, due to the fundamental differences in type and particle size between natural soils and construction waste ag­

that have coupled influence on their breakage mechanisms and patterns.

In this study, “particle breakage” refers to the fracture or fragmentation of a single particle under external loading, which is 
controlled by intrinsic defects, pore structures, and the heterogeneity of internal stress distribution. In contrast, “crushing” describes 
the collective comminution process of multiple particles involving particle–particle interactions and contact stress transfer at the bulk 
scale [8,17,18]. RC and RB aggregates show distinct particle breakage features compared with natural aggregates (NA). RC particles 
contain attached mortar and weak interfacial transition zones (ITZs), leading to progressive crack propagation and layered frag­
mentation. In contrast, RB particles exhibit porous microstructures and pre-existing cracks, resulting in extensive multi-directional 
breakage and lower breakage resistance. Natural aggregates, by comparison, present denser structures and primarily exhibit single 
splitting failure modes (Fig. 1).
According to previous research, the primary constituent materials of construction waste (RC, RB, RM) exhibit distinct breakage 
behaviors, while particle size also significantly influences the breakage strength of construction waste particles. Recent studies have 
advanced the understanding of the single-particle breakage behaviour of construction and demolition waste (CDW) aggregates. Gao 
[12] reported that the breakage strength of recycled concrete and brick aggregates follows a Weibull distribution with a clear size 
dependence. Yosri et al. [54] highlighted that particle breakage of CDW under varying compaction and moisture conditions strongly 
influences their macroscopic strength and stiffness. Moreover, John et al. [16] confirmed that the synergistic interaction of CDW types

2. Single particle compression experiment
2.1. Experimental material
The experimental materials consisted construction and demolition waste (CDW) aggregates sourced from Hangzhou, Zhejiang 
Province, China. Following manual sorting and mechanical processing, three dominant constituents were identified: RC, RB and RM. 
Particle morphology was standardized using the Sneed-Folk classification system [39], with block-shaped particles (axial ratio≤1.5) 
selected based through quantitative shape analysis. The particle size range was determined with reference to Test Methods of Ag­
gregates for Highway Engineering (JTG 3432–2024) and Recycled Coarse Aggregate for Concrete (GB/T 25177–2010). Considering 
experimental operability and uniform loading, all particles were fractionated into three size ranges (4.75–10 mm, 10–20 mm, 
20–25 mm) using ASTM-standard sieves. thirty samples were tested for each particle size group for RC and RB. For RM, thirty-six 
specimens were prepared to account for loading instability, from which thirty valid samples were finally used for data analysis. In 
total 270 dry test specimens (30 per material per size fraction) as illustrated in Fig. 2. This range ensures test stability and data 
comparability. The physical properties of these materials are listed in Table 1.
2.2. Test instrument and scheme
An electronic universal testing machine (Fig. 3) equipped in the laboratory was used to carry out the single-particle compression 
test. A displacement-controlled loading was applied at 1 mm/min loading rate with 50 kN capacity load cell (ISO 7500–1 Class 0.5 
certified), maintaining a force measurement uncertainty with ±0.8 % throughout the tests. Three critical experimental challenges 
were identified: (1) Stress concentration anomalies due to morphological heterogeneity; (2) Premature fracture initiation caused by 
non-uniform stress distribution;(3) Boundary condition artifacts from improper fixturing. To minimize these issues, the shortest axis of 
each particle was first placed perpendicular to the loading platen to ensure stable positioning. Then, the loading beam was manually 
adjustied via remote control until the upper platen was fully in contact with the particle surface. Based on previous studies, the loading 
speed was set at 1 mm/min [5,30,56] to ensure uniform load application and high testing accuracy. For small particle size 
(4.75–10 mm), a metal pad was positioned between the two loading plates and the particles were centred on the pad for loading. A 
camera was used to record the entire breakage process and capture the morphological changes before and after fracture. Finally, the 
test was terminated when the force-displacement curve exhibited a steep drop accompanied by an audible cracking sound, or when the 
load no longer increased with displacement. At this point, visible major fractures were observed, and the bearing capacity had fallen to 
less than approximately 10 % of its peak value, indicating that the particle had reached its breakage strength.

3.2. Particle breakage strength
3.2.1. Effect of particle size on breakage strength
Fig. 5 illustrates the Weibull distributions characterizing the variability in breakage strength of recycled materials across different 
particle size ranges. The maximum characteristic strength σf0 was observed in the 4.75–10 mm size fraction for all material types. A 
progressive decrease in σf0 with increasing particle size was evident, and the 20–25 mm particles exhibited the lowest σf0 values, 
demonstrating pronounced size-dependent behavior consistent with Weibull statistical theory. This phenomenon arises because larger 
particles generally contain a higher density of internal flaws and experience greater stress concentrations during loading [21,22]. On 
the one hand, larger particles typically have more irregular morphologies than smaller ones, with numerous pre-existing cracks and 
pores both on the surface and within the interior, leading to relatively lower strength. Moreover, larger particles require more energy 
to undergo breakage during loading. Once the applied load exceeds the particle’s breakage strength, the release of stored energy

3.2.2. Effect of particle type on breakage strength
Table 2 shows that within the 4.75–25 mm particle size range, the average characteristic strength σf0 of RM, RB, RC were 2.97 MPa, 
5.38 MPa, and 6.28 MPa, respectively. The fractal dimension analysis confirms that RC exhibits the highest σf0, while RM showed the 
lowest σf0, indicating higher susceptibility to breakage. In addition, it can be seen that the average m of RM, RB and RC were 2.73, 2.84 
and 1.38, respectively, reflecting material-dependent variations in distribution of breakage strength. RC exhibited significantly greater 
strength dispersion compared to RM and RB, with this variability becoming more pronounced with increasing particle size. This 
behavior may originate from the heterogeneous type of RC, containing mortar-cement aggregates with rough surfaces and complex 
pore structures [30]. Larger RC particles contain abundant, densely packed pores with non-uniform spatial configurations, creating 
structural heterogeneity within individual particles. Under compressive loading, preferential failure occurs at weakened micro­
structural regions, while less defective zones maintain load-bearing capacity, amplifying strength variability. On the contrary, pore 
structures of RB particles develop increasing uniformity at larger sizes [30], enhancing structural stability and reducing strength 
dispersion, Consequently, RB exhibited the highest average m value.
3.3. Particle breakage mode
Single-particle breakage patterns are governed by material type, morphological characteristics, and particle size. Cai et al. [5]
categorized compressed recycled aggregates particles of different shapes into penetrating fracture, corner rupture, and overall frag­
mentation. Tang et al. [40] identified four distinct failure mechanisms in limestone particulates through coupled mechanical analysis: 
explosive fragmentation, axial splitting, progressive breakage, and localized edge failure. Generally, these breakage modes were 
categorized based on criteria such as the post-breakage particle size and the cracking pattern. However, the breakage process of 
construction waste typically involves multiple concurrent failure mechanisms. Construction waste particles exhibit phase-dependent 
and load-bearing characteristics during fragmentation, with failure mode transitions corresponding to specific energy dissipation 
thresholds, but little attention has been paid to the relationship between particle breakage behavior and the force-displacement 
response, which has received limited attention in previous studies. In this study, combining parameters such as the fractal model of 
the crushed particles, the peak load value, the force-displacement curve (corresponding to the crack propagation of the particles), and 
the range of fragment numbers, the differentiated breakage mechanisms of RC, RB, and RM were analyzed, and the corresponding 
breakage modes under the combined effects of particle size and material type.
Based on the analysis of 270 single-particle compression tests, three parallel tests indicated good repeatability of all particle 
breakage modes, with standard deviations below 10 % and short error bars, demonstrating the good stability of the experimental data.

(Mode II), and bulk breakage (Mode III). Table 3 summarizes the detailed morphological and mechanical characteristics for each

particles (4.75–10 mm) consist primarily of mortar mixed with several small gravel fragments. The complex natural flaws at the in­

multi-peak force-displacement responses, as well as 3–5 major fragments. Since mixed and bulk breakage modes generate more small 
fragments, the D values of RM and RB are larger than those of RC, as shown in Fig. 4.

4. Discussion
This study revealed that particle size and material type jointly govern the breakage behavior of construction waste. The coupling of 
these two factors significantly influences the breakage strength, fractal characteristics, and failure modes of recycled materials. For RC 
particles with high mortar content, smaller particles predominantly undergo bulk breakage, whereas larger ones mainly exhibit in­
termediate splitting. This indicates that optimizing input energy and size gradation can reduce over-pulverization and improve yield 
efficiency. In contrast, RM and RB particles display relatively stable strength-size relationships, suggesting that single-stage crushing 
followed by sieving is sufficient to control particle size and avoid redundant energy consumption. These findings provide practical 
guidance for regulating breakage energy, controlling target particle-size distributions, and improving the efficiency and uniformity of 
recycled aggregate production.
From a material design perspective, distinct breakage patterns and strength distributions among RC, RM, and RB particles reflect 
the decisive role of microstructural features in governing macroscopic mechanical behavior. By tailoring mixture proportions and 
gradation based on each type’s breakage characteristics, recycled concrete and subgrade materials can achieve an optimal balance 
between strength and workability. In particular, enhancing interfacial bonding and controlling coarse aggregate content in RC-based 
materials can reduce splitting failures and improve load-bearing stability.
The present research is limited to single-particle compression tests and does not account for inter-particle interactions, moisture 
effects, or internal defect distributions. Future work will employ CT-based and XRD/XRF quantitative characterization, multi-particle 
contact tests, and numerical simulations to establish the relationships among breakage energy, fragment-size distribution, and fractal 
dimension, incorporate 3D image reconstruction and quantitative morphology parameters (e.g., sphericity, aspect ratio) to further 
evaluate the evolution of particle shape before and after breakage thereby improving the understanding and predictive modeling of 
recycled aggregate breakage mechanisms.
5. Conclusion
This study systematically investigated the breakage behaviors of three kinds of construction waste particles: recycled mortar (RM), 
recycled brick (RB), and recycled concrete (RC), through single-particle compression experiments across varied size fractions 
(4.75–25 mm). The Weibull statistical framework was employed to quantify breakage strength characteristics and strength distri­
bution variability, while fractal dimension analysis of pre- and post-fragmentation gradation curves provided comminution intensity 
quantification. According to the test results, three breakage modes are proposed to describe the breakage behaviors of different 
construction wastes and the coupled effect of particle size and type is carefully analyzed. The principal findings demonstrate: 
(1) The particle size and type jointly exert a coupled effect on the single-particle breakage strength of construction waste. The 
average characteristic strength σf0 of RC particles is approximately twice that of RM particles, representing the highest and 
lowest values, respectively, among the three types of materials. Furthermore, theσf0of all particle types decreases progressively 
with increasing particle size, consistent with the Weibull statistical distribution. The average Weibull modulus m
increases 
with particle size for RM and RB particles, suggesting enhanced strength uniformity. In contrast, RC particles exhibit a 
decreasing m
value with increasing particle size, implying that the structural heterogeneity of RC particles becomes more 
pronounced as the particle size increases.
(2) Particle size and type jointly influence the fractal characteristics of construction waste particles. At the same particle size, the 
average fractal dimension D of RM and RB particles is approximately 10 % higher than that of RC, indicating a higher degree of 
breakage. Considering that RC particles exhibit higher characteristic strength, it can be inferred that RM and RB particles are 
more prone to fragmentation under equivalent loading conditions. Moreover, D increases with particle size for all particle types, 
with the increments in RM and RB being approximately 50 % greater than that of RC, reflecting distinct breakage mechanisms 
among different types of construction waste particles.
(3) Based on the recorded breakage sequences and load–displacement curves, three typical breakage modes of construction waste 
particles were identified: intermediate splitting, mixed breakage, and bulk breakage. Both particle size and type jointly in­
fluence the dominant breakage mode. For RM and RB particles, bulk breakage is predominant when the particle size is small 
(d0≤20 mm). With increasing particle size, the proportion of mixed breakage gradually increases and surpasses that of bulk 
breakage when d0 > 20 mm. In contrast, for RC particles, the three breakage modes occur in approximately equal proportions at 
small sizes (d0≤10 mm), whereas the intermediate splitting mode becomes dominant as particle size increases.
