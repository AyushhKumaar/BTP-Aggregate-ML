Quantifying the influence of single particle shape on crushing 
characteristics of recycled aggregates: Experimental and numerical insights 
Peichen Cai , Xuesong Mao *, Qian Wu *, Yueyue Wang 
College of Highway, Chang’an University, Xi’an 710064, PR China   
A R T I C L E I N F O  
Keywords: 
Particle shapes 
Crushing modes 
Fractal characteristics 
Single-particle crushing strength 
Crushing energy 
Discrete element simulations 
A B S T R A C T  
Exploring the interplay between shapes and crushing characteristics of recycled aggregates (RA) is pivotal for 
understanding their macro-mechanical behavior in bulk materials. This investigation quantitatively characterizes 
the shapes of recycled bricks (RB), recycled concrete (RC), and recycled mortar (RM) particles, and assesses their 
single-particle crushing characteristic. Key focus areas include the analysis of crushing modes, fractal attributes, 
strength, and energy, supplemented by a comparative study using discrete element method (DEM) simulations to 
predict outcomes for particles of specific sphericity. Findings reveal that the shapes of RA particles are mainly 
disk, block, blade and bar, with particle size showing negligible influence on these forms. Crushing behaviors are 
categorized into penetrating fracture, corner rupture, and overall fragmentation, closely linked to particle 
sphericity. Notably, an increase in sphericity leads to a reduction in fractal characteristics of RA, refining the 
gradation distribution from broad to narrow spectrums. An exponential relationship is identified between the 
crushing strength of RA and particle sphericity, a pattern also mirrored in the correlation between crushing 
energy and sphericity, thereby underscoring the significant influence of particle shape. These relationships 
facilitate predictions regarding the crushing strength and energy based on sphericity. While numerical simula­
tions broadly align with experimental data, discrepancies in the crushing energy of highly spheric particles 
necessitate adjustments, resulting in a modified predictive equation for crushing energy. This work enriches the 
understanding of RA crushing behavior, offering invaluable insights for predicting the single-particle strength 
and deformation characteristics across different sphericities, thus advancing material engineering and recycling 
practices.   
1. Introduction 
With the rapid development of highway construction in China, the 
focus has shifted from building new roads to expanding and upgrading 
existing ones, consequently generating a significant amount of con­
struction and demolition waste [1,2]. Among them, the most represen­
tative are bricks, concrete blocks and mortar, accounting for more than 
95 % of the total construction and demolition waste [3]. Reusing recy­
cled bricks (RB), recycled concrete (RB), and recycled mortar (RM) 
processed from construction and demolition waste in infrastructure 
projects is an effective alternative to traditional natural aggregates. This 
approach reduces the construction carbon footprint and achieves addi­
tional economic benefits [4,5]. However, compared to natural gravel 
aggregates, recycled aggregates (RA) exhibit notable differences in 
particle strength and a propensity for crushing [6]. Particle crushing 
directly affects gradation, which in turn influences the overall strength, 
deformation, and permeability characteristics of the materials [7,8]. RA 
particles vary widely in size and shape, making their crushing process 
complex and influenced by numerous factors, among which particle 
shape is a significant determinant [9]. Given that a single particle rep­
resents the smallest fundamental unit of RA, its crushing characteristics 
are crucial for the aggregate’s overall macro-mechanical performance. 
Thus, elucidating the crushing and strength characteristics of RA with 
varying particle shapes is particularly essential for their practical 
application in the construction of highway. 
Current research on single-particle crushing characteristics primarily 
focuses on brittle geomaterial particles, analyzing their crushing modes, 
post-crushing particle size distribution, crushing strength, and influ­
encing factors. Under external load, particles exhibit different crushing 
modes. Tu et al. [10] categorized crushing modes into surface grinding, 
local fracture, complete fracture, and complete breakage based on the 
size variation of resulting fragments. Wang et al. [11] conducted

demonstrating that it is affected by multiple factors, including loading 
conditions, intrinsic particle strength, particle size, and shapes. Among 
these, particle shapes play a significant role in determining the crushing 
modes and strength. 
Thus, while existing studies have produced considerable findings on 
the crushing characteristics of brittle geomaterial particles, the diversity 
of geomaterial particle shapes in practical use presents challenges. On 
one hand, current research has not delved deeply into the relationship 
between particle shape and crushing characteristics, and on the other 
hand, it is difficult to study their macro-mechanical and deformation 
characteristics through indoor experiments. Moreover, the focus of the 
above studies has been mainly on the crushing characteristics of tradi­
tional natural brittle materials, leaving a research gap for new types of 
materials such as those derived from construction and demolition waste, 
which hampers the resource reuse of these materials. To address this, the 
present study first quantitatively characterizes the particle shape of RA 
(RB, RC, and RM), and then conducts single-particle crushing tests on RA 
with different particle shapes (sphericity), investigating their crushing 
modes, fractal characteristics, crushing strength, and changes in crush­
ing energy. Using the discrete element numerical method, the crushing 
process of single particles with different sphericities is simulated and 
compared with experimental data and prediction results to verify the 
reliability of the numerical program. Finally, the program is extended to 
specific particle sphericities to analyze their crushing strength and en­
ergy, providing a theoretical basis for predicting the strength and 
deformation of RA with different sphericity. 
2. Quantitative characterization of RA single particle shapes 
2.1. RA samples and single particle shape indicators 
The materials selected for the experiment consist of recycled aggre­
gates derived from the crushing of building demolition debris, sourced 
from an urban village in Wenling City, Zhejiang Province, China. These 
are primarily composed of RB, RC, and RM, accounting for over 95 % of 
the material, as shown in Fig. 1. The characterization of irregular par­
ticle shapes generally encompasses three levels [22,23]: contour shape, 
concave-convexity, and surface texture. For large and irregular particles 
such as RA, their shape characteristics can be described using two levels: 
contour shape and concave-convexity. In this experiment, a precision 
electronic vernier caliper (accuracy of 0.1 mm) was used to accurately 
measure the length of each RA particle in three mutually perpendicular 
directions: x, y, and z. The maximum value is denoted as L, the inter­
mediate value as I, and the minimum value as S, as shown in Fig. 1(e).

differences of RA particles: elongation index (EI), flatness index (FI), 
Domokos coefficient (Sf), and sphericity (Φ), calculated as per Eqs. (1),

3. Single-particle crushing experiments and discrete element 
modeling 
3.1. Single-particle crushing experiment 
Single-particle crushing tests were conducted on three types of RA. 
The testing apparatus was the MTS universal testing machine in the 
Geotechnical Laboratory of Chang’an University (Fig. 4(a)), with a 
maximum load capacity of 100 kN. The testing machine has a maximum 
load capacity of 100 kN and operates in displacement control mode. 
This study carried out single-particle crushing tests under different 
particle shapes for RB, RC, and RM, with the loading method shown in 
Fig. 4(b). To eliminate the influence of particle size on particle crushing, 
particles with a particle size range of 19–37.5 mm were selected. The 
maximum particle size range of RA was selected because the larger 
particles had a higher crushing probability in the actual engineering 
particle group. The specific experimental design is presented in Table 2, 
totaling 270 particles. For each set, 30 tests were conducted to meet the 
error requirements [24]. Meanwhile, significant differences in the 
sphericity of RA particle shapes were found in the statistics of Section 2. 
Therefore, sphericity (Φ) was chosen as the index for dividing the 
appearance shapes of single particles, and single-particle crushing tests 
were conducted on particles in three categories: sphericity Φ=0.4–0.6 
(Flat spherical), 0.6–0.8 (Subspherical), and 0.8–1.0 (Nearly spherical). 
The specific single-particle crushing test process is as follows.  
● First, before conducting the single-particle crushing test, carefully 
place the particle sample at the center of the bottom plate of the 
universal testing machine to ensure the sample is stable and not tilted 
(optimal stability). Next, slowly adjust the loading plate of the 
testing machine, allowing it to gradually descend until it just makes 
contact with the top surface of the particle sample. Once the loading 
plate is in full contact with the particle surface, set the loading speed 
of the testing machine to a constant rate of 1 mm/min. This ensures 
the acquisition of uniform and continuous force-displacement data. 
Among them, the loading rate setting method follows the approach 
used in References [9,22]. Additionally, using a constant loading rate 
allows for a more focused examination of the influence of particle 
shape on the crushing characteristics of single particles, which is the 
primary focus of this study.  
● Start the testing machine and begin applying compressive load, 
continuously monitoring and recording the force-displacement 
relationship of the particle throughout the compression process 
(Fig. 4c). High-precision sensors and data acquisition systems are 
used to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data. Observe the 
deformation and crushing process of the particle. When the force no 
longer increases during loading, it indicates that the particle has 
reached its crushing limit, at which point the loading is stopped. 
Record the maximum load value and the corresponding displace­
ment value as key indicators for evaluating the crushing strength of 
the particle.  
● After unloading, carefully remove the crushed particle sample and 
inspect and document its morphology for further analysis of the 
crushing mechanism. Export the force-displacement data and

3.2. Discrete element modeling of single-particle crushing 
Using the PFC3D discrete element numerical software, particles of 
different sizes were bonded into a Clump cluster to simulate a single 
particle of RA. To ensure the accuracy of the single particle crushing 
process simulation, the number of sub-particles forming a single RA 
particle was kept above 1500. For simplicity, an ellipsoid was used to 
equivalently simulate the single particle model, consistent with the 
particle shape classification standard in Section 3.1, using sphericity (Φ) 
as the index to divide the appearance shape of the single particle discrete 
element model. The single particle and loading process are illustrated in  
Fig. 5. 
Additionally, the sub-particles composing a single particle were 
bonded using the Bonded Particle Model (BPM) [25], and after particle 
crushing, they degenerated into a linear contact model. In this study, the 
mesoscopic parameters of the numerical model were repeatedly cali­
brated by single-particle crushing test. By adjusting the initial parame­
ters to match the macroscopic characteristics of the RA particles with the 
macroscopic characteristics measured in the laboratory, this method has 
strong applicability [26,27]. The initial test parameters in this study 
mainly refer to Section 3.1 single-particle crushing tests and Literature 
[9,28,29]. Finally, the key mesoscopic parameters of the numerical 
simulation of RB, RC and RM were determined by repeated calculation. 
Specifically, the elastic modulus of RB, RC, and RM are 8.6, 15.2, and 
9.1 GPa, respectively, and the density is 2558, 2860, and 2614 kg/m3, 
respectively, with a Poisson’s ratio of 0.20 for all. In addition, the sur­
face friction coefficient of RA particles used in practical engineering may 
be different, but this study mainly focuses on the influence of particle 
shape on the crushing characteristics, so the friction coefficient can be 
used within a reasonable range. Reference [9,28] shows that the friction 
coefficient of 0.5 is a reasonable value. Furthermore, referring to the 
Literature [17], the bonding strength between sub-particles was set to 
follow a normal distribution. The determination of the mesoscopic pa­
rameters for RB, RC, and RM was strictly based on the results of the 
indoor single-particle crushing tests, with iterative calculations until the 
mechanical response demonstrated by the numerical simulation was 
essentially consistent with the trend reflected by the indoor tests. Take 
an RB particle with d=25.36 mm and sphericity Φ=0.70 as an example, 
as shown in Fig. 6. When a single particle crushes under the action of the 
loading plate, it is the bond between the sub-particles inside the single 
particle that fractures [30]. From Fig. 6, it can be seen that the numerical 
calculation results of single-particle crushing are essentially in agree­
ment with the experimental test results, with only a 0.74 % difference in 
the peak force Ff of particle crushing. This indicates that the use of the 
discrete element numerical method to simulate the single-particle 
crushing process of RA is feasible.

particle sphericity increases, the fractal characteristics of RA weaken, 
and the distribution of gradation narrows from broad, mainly because 
RA with larger sphericity are less completely crushed after reaching

sphericity, resulting in fewer small particles after crushing and thus 
weaker fractal characteristics.

experiments on the crushing of RA with multi-sphericity or in discrete

single particle crushing in RA can be determined based on Eq. (5) and

5. Numerical results and discussion 
By analyzing single-particle crushing experiments of RA with 
different shapes (sphericity Φ), the average crushing strength and en­

element numerical simulation is employed to evaluate the crushing 
strength and energy of single particles with sphericities Φ=0.4–0.6, 
0.6–0.8, and 0.8–1.0, all within a similar particle size range 
(19–37.5 mm). These simulations are then compared with experiments 
to validate the parameters. Subsequently, these parameters are extended 
to single particles of other shapes. Here, Φ=0.40, 0.60, 0.80 and 1.0 are 
taken as examples (but not limited to this), and their crushing properties 
are simulated and compared with the predictions of Eqs. (8) and (10). 
5.1. Single-particle crushing process 
In order to explain key processes such as single-particle crushing 
development and fracture evolution in detail, RB particles with a 
sphericity of Φ = 0.70 are selected as typical representatives in this 
section. The evolution process of particle crushing is shown in Figs. 15 
and 16, and the corresponding stress-strain curve is shown in Fig. 17. 
This choice is primarily based on the following two reasons. First, the 
sphericity of Φ = 0.70 lies in the middle of the sphericity range (0.4–1.0) 
examined in this study, making it a better representative of the crushing 
behavior across different sphericities. Second, given that the simulation 
processes across all sphericities exhibit similar overall trends, selecting 
an instance with intermediate sphericity helps avoid lengthiness and 
repetition. 
Figs. 15(a) and 16(a) show the particle just before the start of 
loading, in contact with the loading plate (ending the pre-loading state), 
with only a few fragments and noticeable force chains distributed at 
both ends of the loading plate and base. This corresponds to point a in 
the stress-strain curve in Fig. 17. As loading begins, initially, there is no 
significant change in the appearance and force state of the particle, as 
shown in Fig. 15(b) and point b in Fig. 17, though the number of frag­
ments and force chains increases internally compared to the previous 
stage (Fig. 16(b)). Subsequently, cracks start to appear after a period of 
loading (Fig. 15(c)), and the first peak on the stress-strain curve is 
observed at point c in Fig. 17. At this stage, some fragments penetrate 
through, with force chains mainly concentrated around the fragments 
(Fig. 16(c)). After the first peak, the particle undergoes breaking, leading 
to the second (point d in Fig. 17) and third (point e in Fig. 17) peak 
points. The corresponding particle appearance and the distribution of 
internal fragments and force chains expand further (Fig. 16(d)(e) and 15 
(d)(e)). The particle is essentially crushed upon reaching the system’s 
maximum stress peak (point f in Fig. 17), with complete penetration of 
fragments and an expanded range of force chains (Fig. 16(f)), eventually 
breaking into 3–4 pieces, as shown in Fig. 15(g)-(j) and points g-j in 
Fig. 17. After peak f, the distribution of fragments and force chains ex­
pands from the penetrating central crack towards the right side (Fig. 16 
(g)-(j)), indicating that after complete breaking, the largest fragment on 
the right side bears the main load, leading to further internal cracking. 
Comparing the stress-strain curves of the single particle crushing test 
results (left side of Fig. 17) reveals that the numerical simulation results 
of this study basically match the experimental test results, with only a 
1.05 % difference in the stress peak values. Additionally, the post- 
crushing crushing mode of the numerical simulation closely aligns 
with those observed in the experimental load crushing tests (Section 4.1, 
distribution of crushing), as shown on the right side of Fig. 17 (top view). 
This further demonstrates the feasibility of the numerical simulation 
method presented in this study.

sults of individual experiments may vary due to various factors, 
considering a larger number of samples, shows that these results tend to 
be consistent, reflecting general stability in the relationship between 
crushing energy and particle sphericity Φ. Therefore, the numerical 
program can also be used to simulate and calculate the crushing energy 
of particles with other sphericities. 
5.3. Discrete element simulation of single-particle crushing with different 
shapes and correction of prediction results 
The study conducted simulations using the PFC3D discrete element 
numerical program to calculate the crushing outcomes for single parti­
cles of RB, RC, and RM with sphericities Φ=0.40, 0.60, 0.80, and 1.0. 
The results are presented in Figs. 20 and 21. Fig. 20 compares the 
average crushing strength of single particles of RB, RC, and RM obtained 
from numerical results with those predicted by Eq. (8). It was found that, 
under specific sphericities Φ, the numerical results for average crushing 
strength are in good agreement with those obtained from Eq. (8). Under 
conditions of equivalent particle size, the greater the particle sphericity 
Φ, the greater the sustained crushing force, and correspondingly, the 
greater the crushing strength σf. Thus, the particle sphericity Φ and Eq. 
(8) can be used to predict the crushing strength of single particles with 
any sphericity, but the specific results may still require further experi­
mental validation.

correlation coefficient R2 is generally low, and the fitting correlation 
coefficients R2 of RB, RC and RM numerical results are 0.61, 0.54 and 
0.77, respectively. Specifically, the predictions tend to overestimate

6. Conclusion and outlook 
6.1. Research conclusion 
This paper first quantitatively characterizes the shape characteristics 
of RA particles and then studies the crushing modes, fractal character­
istics, particle crushing strength, and crushing energy of single particles 
with different shapes (sphericity Φ) through single-particle crushing 
experiments involving RB, RC, and RM. Finally, by comparing and 
analyzing the experimental and predicted results through PFC3D discrete 
element numerical simulation, it predicts the crushing strength and 
crushing energy of particles with specific sphericity. The main conclu­
sions are as follows. 
(1) The shapes of RA particles are mainly disk, block, blade and bar. 
The flatness of the RA particles used in this study ranges from 0.30 to 
0.96, the elongation ratio ranges from 0.29 to 0.98, the Domokos coef­
ficient Sf ranges from 3.03 to 6.58, and the sphericity ranges from 0.42 to 
0.93. The particle size of the RA has essentially no effect on their shape. 
(2) The crushing modes of RB, RC, and RM particles can be divided 
into penetrating fractures, corner rupture, and overall fragmentation, 
with a correlation between the single-particle crushing mode and par­
ticle sphericity. The proportion of penetrating fractures and corner 
rupture tends to decrease with increasing sphericity, while the propor­
tion of overall fragmentation tends to increase. 
(3) Under different particle sphericities, the fractal dimensions of RB 
and RM are higher than those of RC, and the fractal dimensions of the 
three types of RA decrease with increasing particle sphericity, indicating 
a reduced degree of crushing. As the sphericity increases, the fractal 
characteristics of the RA weaken, and the gradation distribution nar­
rows. The final fractal dimensions of multi-sphericity RB, RC, and RM 
are 2.47, 2.31, and 2.49, respectively. 
(4) The particle crushing strength of RC under the same sphericity is 
higher than that of RB and RM. With the increase of particle sphericity, 
the crushing strength of RA shows an increasing trend, following an 
exponential growth model. Similarly, the relationship between particle 
crushing energy and particle sphericity also follows an exponential 
function, showing a clear effect of particle shape. The crushing strength 
and crushing energy of a single RA particle can be predicted by its 
sphericity. 
(5) The results of the discrete element numerical simulation show 
that the single-particle crushing strength of RA can be calculated 
through sphericity and Eq. (8). For single-particle crushing energy with 
larger sphericities (Φ≥0.8), Eq. (11) is superior to Eq. (10), thus its 
crushing energy can be calculated through sphericity and Eq. (11), and 
further validation through experiments is needed. 
6.2. Research limitations and outlook 
Additionally, while this study has yielded significant findings, there 
are still limitations that need to be addressed and several avenues for 
future research. 
(1) Three materials are selected in this study, and there are some 
limitations in the types of materials. Future research should be extended 
to other types of construction waste to verify the universality of the 
conclusions.

(2) The current research does not fully cover all possible particle size 
distributions, and a wider range should be considered in the future to 
improve the applicability of the research results. 
(3) This study mainly studied the influence of particle shape on the 
crushing characteristics, using a single loading rate and method, without 
considering the complex actual working conditions. In the future, more 
diversified loading conditions and environmental factors should be 
studied to fully understand the mechanical behavior of RA.

characteristics and mechanical properties of RA, thereby enhancing its 
potential for application in engineering practice.
