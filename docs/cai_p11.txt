5. Numerical results and discussion 
By analyzing single-particle crushing experiments of RA with 
different shapes (sphericity Φ), the average crushing strength and en­
ergy can be predicted using Eqs. (8) and (10). The PFC3D discrete 
element numerical simulation is employed to evaluate the crushing 
strength and energy of single particles with sphericities Φ=0.4–0.6, 
0.6–0.8, and 0.8–1.0, all within a similar particle size range 
(19–37.5 mm). These simulations are then compared with experiments 
to validate the parameters. Subsequently, these parameters are extended 
to single particles of other shapes. Here, Φ=0.40, 0.60, 0.80 and 1.0 are 
taken as examples (but not limited to this), and their crushing properties 
are simulated and compared with the predictions of Eqs. (8) and (10). 
5.1. Single-particle crushing process 
In order to explain key processes such as single-particle crushing 
development and fracture evolution in detail, RB particles with a 
sphericity of Φ = 0.70 are selected as typical representatives in this 
section. The evolution process of particle crushing is shown in Figs. 15 
and 16, and the corresponding stress-strain curve is shown in Fig. 17. 
This choice is primarily based on the following two reasons. First, the 
sphericity of Φ = 0.70 lies in the middle of the sphericity range (0.4–1.0) 
examined in this study, making it a better representative of the crushing 
behavior across different sphericities. Second, given that the simulation 
processes across all sphericities exhibit similar overall trends, selecting 
an instance with intermediate sphericity helps avoid lengthiness and 
repetition. 
Figs. 15(a) and 16(a) show the particle just before the start of 
loading, in contact with the loading plate (ending the pre-loading state), 
with only a few fragments and noticeable force chains distributed at 
both ends of the loading plate and base. This corresponds to point a in 
the stress-strain curve in Fig. 17. As loading begins, initially, there is no 
significant change in the appearance and force state of the particle, as 
shown in Fig. 15(b) and point b in Fig. 17, though the number of frag­
ments and force chains increases internally compared to the previous 
stage (Fig. 16(b)). Subsequently, cracks start to appear after a period of 
loading (Fig. 15(c)), and the first peak on the stress-strain curve is 
observed at point c in Fig. 17. At this stage, some fragments penetrate 
through, with force chains mainly concentrated around the fragments 
(Fig. 16(c)). After the first peak, the particle undergoes breaking, leading 
to the second (point d in Fig. 17) and third (point e in Fig. 17) peak 
points. The corresponding particle appearance and the distribution of 
internal fragments and force chains expand further (Fig. 16(d)(e) and 15 
(d)(e)). The particle is essentially crushed upon reaching the system’s 
maximum stress peak (point f in Fig. 17), with complete penetration of 
fragments and an expanded range of force chains (Fig. 16(f)), eventually 
breaking into 3–4 pieces, as shown in Fig. 15(g)-(j) and points g-j in 
Fig. 17. After peak f, the distribution of fragments and force chains ex­
pands from the penetrating central crack towards the right side (Fig. 16 
(g)-(j)), indicating that after complete breaking, the largest fragment on 
the right side bears the main load, leading to further internal cracking. 
Comparing the stress-strain curves of the single particle crushing test 
results (left side of Fig. 17) reveals that the numerical simulation results 
of this study basically match the experimental test results, with only a 
1.05 % difference in the stress peak values. Additionally, the post- 
crushing crushing mode of the numerical simulation closely aligns 
with those observed in the experimental load crushing tests (Section 4.1, 
distribution of crushing), as shown on the right side of Fig. 17 (top view). 
This further demonstrates the feasibility of the numerical simulation 
method presented in this study.
